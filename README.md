### A visualization of Typhoon Yolanda's aid network. 

#### Motivation

The lack of coordination between stakeholders causes inequality between demand points, or province-level units that receive aid. Relief efforts were concentrated in high-profile locations while other areas were neglected. The city of Tacloban (Leyte) was a focal point of international aid. Meanwhile, outlying areas in northwestern Cebu (e.g., Bantayan Island) suffered comparable devastation (up to 90% of structures damaged) but saw delayed and limited aid. Local officials in Cebu lamented that so much relief was diverted to Leyte that communities in Cebu’s islands were not prioritized despite their desperate needs (Devex, 2013). Evaluations later confirmed that response activities tend to cluster around major coordination hubs, leaving remote demand points waiting much longer for assistance. The lack of a coordinated geographic coverage plan led to overlap in some locations and gaps in others, exacerbating the inequality between well-served and poorly-served communities.

#### Result

From this visualization, I argue that inequality is not only an operational failure but an inevitable outcome from aid organizations’ incentives and constraints. I chose to investigate equality in short-term cash assistance (throughout November 2013), since it is the most well-documented indicator of aid. Other metrics, such as emergency kits and food, are difficult to keep consistent and often not officially reported.
